About

Computer science taught me to solve the problem. Philosophy taught me to check it's the right one.

I'm a computer science major with a philosophy minor at Duke. CS is the hard side of it — linear thinking, decomposition, getting a thing to actually work. Philosophy is the other half: framing the question, building the argument, noticing where one quietly falls apart. They sound unrelated and they aren't. Both are about solving problems and understanding something well enough to explain it. They just disagree about where the difficulty lives.

Both halves show up in the work. At Deloitte this past summer I worked a multibillion-dollar bank carve-out, where a tracker several workstreams depended on was quietly miscounting applications. Not because anyone was careless — the tagging logic simply didn't mean what everyone assumed it meant. Noticing it was the easy part. Getting it corrected, and making sure it couldn't silently drift again, was the real work.

I want to build products because that instinct is the job description: sit between the people who know what's technically possible and the people who know what's actually needed, and decide what's worth doing first. I'm technical enough to know what I'm asking engineers for, and I'd rather ship the small right thing than the big plausible one.

Away from that, I'm a volunteer firefighter and I cook seven-course tasting menus for sixteen people. Both are the same exercise: a plan, a clock, and no room for a step out of order.

01 Product · Team project

FridgeHero

A food-tracking app for families, taken from user interviews through to a North Star metric and a go-to-market plan.

User researchFigma MakeLovableGTM

At a glance

Course
Applied Product Management, Duke
Research
10 in-depth user interviews, 3 proto-persona archetypes
Prototype
Figma Make + Lovable
North Star
70%+ food saved per user

American households throw out 30–40% of the food they buy — about $2,900 a year for a family of four. The reason isn't that people don't care. It's that a fridge has no interface. You can't see what's in it, when it expires, or what you already bought last week.

We ran ten in-depth user interviews and mapped three proto-persona archetypes before designing anything. That pointed at Jessica: 34, two kids, full-time job, the primary grocery shopper in her household. Mothers are the primary shopper in roughly 80% of households and families with kids waste the most food per capita, so she had both the highest waste and the strongest financial reason to change.

Mapping her food lifecycle across five stages surfaced two pain points worth building for: over-purchasing (buying “just in case,” swayed by bulk deals, no visibility into current stock) and no visibility (forgetting what's in the fridge, buying duplicates, no expiration tracking). One interview line stuck with us — “I'll forget about having a food item, so it might be hidden in the fridge, and I just don't see it.”

The product answers those directly: a receipt scanner so tracking costs no effort, a food tracker she checks before shopping, expiry reminders, AI recipes that use what's about to go bad, and a stats page showing the money and waste she's avoided.

Metrics we'd hold ourselves to

  • 70%+Food saved per user — North Star
  • 10KWeekly active users by month 6
  • 40%D7 retention
  • $40Saved per user per month

Monetization is freemium — core tracking free, with premium analytics, meal planning, family sharing and grocery integrations. Acquisition targets where the persona already is: parent groups on Facebook and Instagram, mom and parent influencers, coupon and meal-planning communities, and grocery-adjacent touchpoints.

Built with two teammates for Applied Product Management at Duke. We split the work by phase rather than by function, so all three of us had a hand in the research, the product decisions and the deck.

02 Product · Coursework

PlateBudget In progress

A weekly meal planner that designs seven dinners around a grocery budget, prices the list against a real store, and tells you what to swap when the week runs over.

Next.jsTypeScriptTailwindDockerClaude APIKroger API

At a glance

Status

Model chain

Pricing

localStorage only — no database, no account

You tell it what you like, what you can spend, and how many people you're feeding. It designs seven dinners that deliberately share ingredients, consolidates them into one shopping list, prices that list against your actual Harris Teeter, and if the week runs over budget it proposes specific swaps to get back under.

The decision I'd defend hardest is showing the budget as a range rather than a number, and widening that range as more of the list falls back to estimated prices. A single total would look more authoritative and be less true. A mostly-estimated week should not present the same confidence as a fully-priced one, so the interface makes its own uncertainty visible instead of hiding it behind a tidy figure.

Everything is a fallback chain. The model chain runs Claude first, then GPT-5.1, then any OpenAI-compatible endpoint, all sharing byte-identical prompts and one Zod schema so the output shape can't drift between providers. The Kroger price provider never throws — missing credentials, no store near your ZIP, no product match, a timeout, a 5xx all resolve to “no quote for this item” and fall through to estimates. The consequence is the one I wanted: the app is fully usable before you have any store credentials at all.

Ingredient matching is where I made a deliberately narrow choice. Words like ground, dried, frozen, diced are specifically not stripped during matching, because ground beef is not beef and a can of diced tomatoes is not a fresh tomato. Being cleverer there would have produced a shorter list and a wrong one.

My favourite bug: Harris Teeter's chain code in Kroger's Locations API is HART, not HARRISTEETER. The wrong value isn't rejected — it returns 200 with zero stores. So the store picker silently empties, every price quietly falls back to an estimate, and nothing anywhere reports an error. A success response that means failure is the same shape of problem as the miscounting tracker I found at Deloitte, and I seem to keep finding them.

The README carries a known-limitations section naming the ways the estimate is wrong, including that unreconcilable pack sizes are priced as a single package and therefore bias the budget low. I'd rather ship a number with its error bars stated than one that looks clean.

Coursework for CompSci 390 at Duke, and still being built — a demo video is coming. The source lives on Duke's internal GitLab, so there's no public link yet; happy to walk through the code or demo it live.

03 Machine learning · Team project

Teaching an agent to drive from pixels

Deep RL agents that learn Super Mario Kart from raw frames, with no hand-crafted features. I built the PPO side.

PythonPyTorchGymnasiumLuaDocker

The question was whether an agent could learn to drive from nothing but the pixels on screen, and how two very different algorithms compare when you do. We wrote a Deep Q-Network and Proximal Policy Optimization from scratch rather than importing them, ran both on two tracks, and benchmarked them against each other.

I owned the PPO agent — actor-critic, generalized advantage estimation, the clipped surrogate objective, and entropy scheduling — along with every PPO training run and the evaluation and plotting pipeline the whole comparison rests on. My teammate built the DQN, the Gymnasium wrappers, and the Lua reward integration that pulls checkpoint progress straight out of game RAM.

The interesting part wasn't the algorithms, it was the reward function. Our first design terminated episodes on collision, and the agents figured out they could idle next to a wall to farm episode length without ever driving. Switching to progress-based termination improved throughput roughly tenfold. Log-transforming rewards was what finally stabilized PPO training.

DQN won by a wide margin on raw return — 7,789 against PPO's 338 — because experience replay let it revisit the rare lap-completion events that PPO saw once and moved past. But on a track it had never seen, DQN transferred poorly while PPO held about 55% of its return. The headline number and the useful number pointed in opposite directions, which is the finding I'd actually defend.

We catalogued four distinct failure modes, including “replay buffer collapse” — once the buffer fills with only perfect runs, the agent forgets how to recover from a mistake.

04 Game · Personal build

Break//Out

A browser brickbreaker with levels, lives and a high score that survives a refresh. Playable right here, nothing to install.

JavaScriptCanvasHTMLCSS

I wanted to find out how much of a game being fun comes from features and how much comes from tuning. The answer is mostly tuning. Brickbreaker is a deliberately small target — a paddle, a ball, a grid — which makes it a good place to test that, because there is nowhere to hide behind scope.

It runs on a canvas render loop with paddle physics, brick grids that rebuild per level, lives, pause, and a best score kept in localStorage so it persists between sessions. The ball's angle off the paddle depends on where it hits, which is the single change that made it feel like a game rather than a physics demo.

The bug worth mentioning: after you lost a life, there was no way to relaunch the ball. The game still rendered, the HUD still updated, everything looked alive — it was simply unplayable from that point on. You only catch it by deliberately losing, which is not what you do when you are testing something you just built and want to work.

That is the same failure I keep meeting in professional work: the happy path is fine, and the thing nobody exercises is quietly broken. I fixed it, and then I stopped — it is a finished toy rather than a finished product, and I would rather say so than pretend otherwise.
